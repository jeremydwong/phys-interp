1What changed since InceptionV1

The essay's thesis is that most of what looked like superposition (neurons arbitrarily packing unrelated features) was really two ordinary problems: an architecture that learned poorly, and tools that rendered neurons badly. Fix both and the same-size network looks clean. The residual mess is mostly what Cammarata calls recursive generalization: a neuron that detects a V-shape grows to fire on cat ears and car fronts because those share a shape, not because the network ran out of room.

The headline numbers for InceptionV1's last layer: about 5% of neurons interpretable with 2023 tools, about 50% with better visualization, about 70% with modern regularization, and about 99% in a ConvNeXt of the same size (7.06M parameters) trained on the same data. On Qwen3-32B he reports, as preliminary, roughly 90% of neuron poles doing one main job.

1.1 The architecture: “residual connections, LayerNorm, and a couple other improvements”

The essay never names the other improvements. The modern model it compares against is ConvNeXt, and the ConvNeXt paper documents exactly which changes it made, one at a time, starting from a ResNet. So the list below is the documented difference between ConvNeXt and a 2014-era network like InceptionV1. The right-hand column is my reading of why each might matter for interpretability; the essay only makes that argument for residuals.

The general lesson. Nobody designed these changes for interpretability. They were designed to make deep networks train well, and cleaner neurons came along with that. For your project this means: don't build an exotic “interpretable” architecture. Build a well-trained, conventional one, and choose the conventional options that keep a privileged neuron basis and separate the kinds of mixing.

1.2 The tools

  • Feature visualization that works. The original 2017 method lacked transformation robustness (jittering, scaling and rotating the image while optimizing it). Adding it made early neurons crisp. Deep-layer visualizations were still poor because the regularization settings were slightly off. In deep layers the optimizer “takes the easiest path, which in InceptionV1 often means dogs.” Small tuning fixed it.
  • Dataset examples at scale. Rank the top activating examples over the whole training set (50,000 images in the essay) and read raw activation shapes, not only percentiles.
  • Read the negative pole. A neuron's most negative examples tell you which distinction it draws. Examples from the essay: taxis versus ambulances, wolves versus huskies. A label from the positive side alone can mislead.
  • Watch neurons grow over training. Checkpoint replays show a neuron start on one concept and spread to related ones. This is the evidence for recursive generalization over arbitrary packing.
  • Steering and ablation. If you think a neuron means X, push it up and check that the output changes in an X-like way. Then ablate the circuits it reads from.
  • Neuron-level attribution graphs. Trace which upstream neurons drive a given activation, and test the trace with ablation.
  • Agents in the loop. 2023 automated explainers scored neurons by how well a short label predicts activations. The essay argues this rewarded vague labels. Agents that propose a hypothesis, build test inputs and check them close much of the gap.
  • Sparse autoencoders as a check, not a prerequisite. On the last ConvNeXt layer about 88% of SAE activity was near-copies of single neurons, and removing the remaining dictionary directions didn't change accuracy. SAEs helped most on the smallest model, whose neurons were coarse.

1.3 Why this matters for dynamics

One finding transfers directly. The famous “car–dog superposition” circuit from Zoom In turns out to be pose: neurons that group cars and dogs by the way they face the camera. These pose neurons persist in DINOv2 and in ConvNeXt at several sizes. A vision network trained only on still images already builds abstract geometric variables that cut across object categories. The bet behind your project is that a network trained to predict motion will build the same kind of abstract variables for physics: contact, impulse, support, and torque transmitted through a linkage.

2Architectures built to be read

There are two routes, and you should do both. Pretrained video models tell you what large models trained on real video actually learned, but you never get ground-truth forces for the clips. Small models trained from scratch on simulation give you every force, torque and contact at every frame, so you can check any neuron against the truth. The simulated route is where you will get clean results first.

A physics catch to design around. A camera sees kinematics, not forces. A model can only represent force as something like mass × acceleration, or by recognizing contexts in which forces occur (contact, a taut rope, a supporting surface). A neuron that correlates with force might just track acceleration. So build stimulus pairs where the kinematics match and the forces differ (same trajectory, different mass, and therefore different force) and pairs where the forces match and the kinematics differ. Only a neuron that follows force across both pairs is a force neuron.

2.1 Design principles taken from §1

  • Residual stream with pre-LayerNorm. Blocks write deltas, and you can read the stream between any two blocks.
  • Wide, single-nonlinearity MLPs (ReLU or GELU). Start without gated units like SwiGLU. Gated neurons are readable, but the essay found a “double-negative” regime where both halves are negative and the product is positive, which you then have to account for.
  • Factorize the mixing. Keep spatial, temporal and channel mixing in separate layers, the same way ConvNeXt separates spatial from channel mixing. Then a temporal-mixing layer is the only place where velocity and acceleration can be computed, and you know where to look.
  • Causal in time. Each timestep's activations depend only on the past, so you can align a neuron's response to the contact frame without leakage from the future.
  • Entities and pairs as separate things. Interactions happen between pairs of objects or pairs of linked segments. An architecture with explicit pairwise “edges” gives interaction variables a natural home.
  • Conventional, modern training. AdamW, weight decay, augmentation and a long enough schedule. Under-trained models look messy for reasons that have nothing to do with superposition.

2.2 Three candidate models to train from scratch

A · Slot interaction net ~5–20M params

A ConvNeXt-style frame encoder, then Slot Attention to split each frame into K object slots, then an Interaction Network core that computes a message for every pair of slots with a wide ReLU MLP. It predicts the next frame's slots.

Why: Cranmer et al. (2020) trained exactly this kind of edge-message network on particle systems and found the messages were linear transforms of the true pairwise forces. They then recovered the force law symbolically. This is the strongest prior result that “force neurons” exist and can be found.

B · Factorized space-time ViT ~10–30M params

Patch tokens per frame. Each block has spatial attention within a frame, causal temporal attention across frames at the same location, and a wide GELU MLP. Pre-LN residual stream. Predict the next frame's latents (JEPA-style) or pixels.

Why: it has no built-in objects or pairs. Compare it with A. If it invents contact and force variables on its own, that is a stronger finding. It is also a small version of V-JEPA 2, so what you learn here carries over to the pretrained model.

C · State-space dynamics model ~1–5M params

Skip pixels. Feed joint angles and velocities (or object states) into a small residual MLP or transformer that predicts the next state. Use this for the two-link arm and humanoid experiments.

Why: it is the fastest way to answer whether a dynamics predictor builds an interaction-torque variable. It trains in minutes, and every quantity in the equations of motion is available as ground truth.

2.5 Interaction torques as a concrete target

For a planar two-link arm (shoulder angle q₁, elbow angle q₂), the shoulder torque is

τ₁ = M₁₁(q₂)·q̈₁  +  M₁₂(q₂)·q̈₂  −  h(q₂)·(2q̇₁q̇₂ + q̇₂²)
M₁₂ = I₂ + m₂·l₁·lc2·cos q₂     h = m₂·l₁·lc2·sin q₂

The highlighted terms are the interaction torque: the load the shoulder feels because the elbow moved. This is a good probe target because it is nonlinear in observable kinematics (products of velocities, multiplied by trig functions of posture). A neuron that tracks it linearly cannot be a copy of any single input; the model had to compute it. Compare against probes for the simpler terms (q̈₁ alone, q̇₂² alone) to show the model is tracking the interaction and not one of its ingredients.

2.6 Gravity vector extraction

Gravity is the one force that is always present, so it makes a good first target. The question is whether the model holds an explicit estimate of “which way is down” and “how strong is gravity,” and whether it uses that estimate when it predicts motion. A video model has three kinds of evidence for it:

  • Dynamic cues. Free-falling and thrown objects share one constant acceleration. Its direction is down in the image, and its size (in pixels per frame²) gives g once scale is known.
  • Static cues. Floors, horizons, vertical edges of walls and doors, things resting on surfaces, hanging ropes. A single frame is enough. Image models like DINOv2 almost certainly carry some of this, since camera up-vector estimation from single images (for example Perspective Fields, CVPR 2023) works well.
  • Body cues. How people and animals lean, brace and balance. This connects to the pose route.

What to look for

Direction in the image plane is an angle, so expect it to be encoded the way head-direction cells encode heading: a population of neurons each with a preferred direction and roughly cosine tuning, not a single “gravity neuron.” Concretely:

  • Probe for (cos θ, sin θ) of gravity's angle in the image, not θ itself, since the angle wraps around. A two-output linear probe on a layer either recovers the angle well or it doesn't.
  • Fit tuning curves per neuron. Sweep camera roll through 360° and fit a von Mises curve to each neuron. Plot the distribution of preferred directions. A uniform spread suggests a population code for the full circle; a cluster at 270° (image-down) suggests the model has only learned “down usually means down the screen.”
  • Probe magnitude separately. Simulate the same scenes at Earth, Moon and 2× Earth gravity and probe for g. Direction and magnitude may live in different places.
  • Check equivariance. Rotate the whole input by α. A real gravity representation rotates its probe readout by α too. A representation tied to image axes won't.

Stimuli that separate the cues

  • Rolled camera. The easiest manipulation in MuJoCo or Kubric: roll the camera so gravity points anywhere in the image, with all cues agreeing.
  • Cue conflict. Tilt the room geometry one way and the physics gravity vector another (simulators allow arbitrary gravity vectors). Which cue does the probe follow? Does that change over the clip as falling objects reveal the true direction? This is the same experiment as a tilted-room illusion in human perception.
  • Gravito-inertial ambiguity. An accelerating camera produces the same image-plane acceleration of a dropped object as tilted gravity. This is the otolith problem from vestibular physiology. Compare clips where the camera accelerates against clips where gravity tilts, and check whether the model separates them using static cues.
  • No dynamic cue. Static scenes, or only objects resting on surfaces. If the probe still works, the estimate comes from layout, not from watching things fall.

Testing that the model uses it

Find the probe direction for gravity angle, then add a rotated version of it to the residual stream (steering) and roll the predictor forward. If dropped objects in the predicted frames now fall sideways, the representation is causal. In the state-only arm model (candidate C) the matching target is the gravity torque, which you can probe alongside the interaction torque:

G₁(q) = (m₁·lc1 + m₂·l₁)·g·cos q₁  +  m₂·lc2·g·cos(q₁ + q₂)

Train with gravity direction randomized per episode and given only implicitly (through the motion), and the model has to infer g from the first few frames and hold it. That gives you an inferred latent you can locate, the same way you would locate a context variable in a language model.

2.7 The experiment loop

  1. Catalogue. For every neuron in the chosen layers, store top and bottom activating clips and frames. Align each clip's activations to a physical event (contact onset, peak force) and plot the event-aligned average, the way a neurophysiologist builds a peri-stimulus time histogram.
  2. Correlate. Regress each neuron, and linear probes on the whole layer, against the ground-truth variables: contact flag, impulse magnitude, net force, interaction torque, plus the kinematic confounds (velocity, acceleration, distance to nearest object).
  3. Contrast. Run the matched pairs: collision vs. near-miss with identical approach, same trajectory with heavy vs. light objects. A contact neuron should split the first pair; a force neuron should split the second.
  4. Intervene. Clamp or amplify the candidate neuron and roll the predictor forward. A contact neuron pushed high during a near-miss should make the predicted trajectory bounce. Without this step you have a correlate, not a mechanism.
  5. Trace and replay. Find which upstream neurons feed it (attribution plus ablation), read its negative pole, and replay its tuning across training checkpoints to see what it started as.

3.3 Storage budget

Video activations are large. For ViT-L on a 16-frame 256px clip: 8 temporal × 16 × 16 spatial = 2,048 tokens, times 4,096 MLP neurons, times 2 bytes = about 17 MB per layer per clip. A thousand clips across all 24 layers is about 400 GB, more than your free disk. So:

  • Pick 3–4 layers (early, middle, late) rather than all of them.
  • Store per-neuron summaries for the full set (max, mean, the event-aligned time course pooled over space) and keep full tensors only for each neuron's top clips.
  • Write with zarr in fp16 so you can read slices without loading everything.

3.4 Practical notes for MPS

  • Use bf16 or fp16; fp32 doubles memory for no interpretability benefit at inference.
  • FlashAttention isn't available. attn_implementation="sdpa" works and is the default in recent transformers.
  • Run one clip at a time for the 7–8B VLMs. Batch small vision models freely.
  • If something is slow or fails on MPS, run it on CPU before suspecting your code. The CPU side of the M4 Max is fast enough for small models.

6.0 Notation and the interpretability ladder

Write $x$ for an input (image or token context), $z_j(x)$ for the pre-activation of neuron $j$, and $a_j(x)=\phi(z_j(x))$ for its activation. For convolutional channels, a per-image score is the spatial maximum. The essay gives four estimates for the share of interpretable neurons in InceptionV1's last layer. Read as a sequence, they split the gain into tools, training recipe and architecture.

$$\underbrace{\sim 5\%}_{\text{2014 weights, 2017 tools}} \;\xrightarrow{\;\Delta_{\text{tools}}\approx +45\;}\; \underbrace{\sim 50\%}_{\text{same weights, fixed feature vis + attribution}} \;\xrightarrow{\;\Delta_{\text{recipe}}\approx +20\;}\; \underbrace{\sim 70\%}_{\text{retrained, modern regularization}} \;\xrightarrow{\;\Delta_{\text{arch}}\approx +29\;}\; \underbrace{\sim 99\%}_{\text{ConvNeXt, 7.06M params}}$$

The additive split is our reading. The essay gives only the four levels, and the steps are not shown to be independent or to commute. The essay's own wording: "I put it around 5% with tools of that era, others at ~0%. With better interpretability tools (fixed feature visualization, methods for visualizing attribution etc) it's more like 50% on the same network ... Retraining the same architecture with more reasonable modern regularization it's more like 70%. In the ConvNeXt of the same size, it's about 99%."

Source: Cammarata 2026, "Neural Networks Are Already Clean", https://already-clean.goodfire.com/ (section "A History of Microscope Errors").

6.1 Architecture

6.1.1 Residual stream: blocks write deltas

InceptionV1 is a plain feed-forward stack. Each layer's output is the only path forward, so its channels must carry everything later layers need. A residual network adds each block's output to a running stream.

$$\text{plain (InceptionV1):}\quad x_{\ell+1}=F_\ell(x_\ell) \qquad\qquad \text{residual (ConvNeXt):}\quad x_{\ell+1}=x_\ell+\gamma_\ell\odot F_\ell(x_\ell)$$
$$x_L = x_0+\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\gamma_\ell\odot F_\ell(x_\ell),\qquad \frac{\partial x_L}{\partial x_\ell}= I+\sum_{m=\ell}^{L-1}\frac{\partial\,(\gamma_m\odot F_m(x_m))}{\partial x_\ell}$$

The identity term gives every block a direct gradient path and a direct read path to the output. The "delta" argument, as we formalize it: in a plain net, layer $\ell$'s $C_\ell$ channels are a bottleneck. By the data-processing inequality, $I(x_{\ell+1};y)\le I(a_\ell;y)$, so every downstream-relevant distinction must be re-encoded in $a_\ell$, and neurons must relay information they do not themselves compute. In a residual net, the block's hidden units only need to encode the update $F_\ell(x_\ell)$, while unchanged information passes through on the identity. That removes the relay pressure the essay calls "overflow". The essay states the empirical side. The residual stream "was introduced to help learn deeper neural networks, and it also seems to help the deeper layers be conceptually clean". "Architectural problems made later layers overflow." Its compression tests deliberately remove the bypass: "crush the last layer, stop it from routing around the residual stream".

Source: https://already-clean.goodfire.com/ ("The Short Version"; "What Neurons Do Under Pressure"); He et al. 2016 (ResNet).

6.1.2 The ConvNeXt block (depthwise conv, inverted bottleneck, GELU, layer scale)

ConvNeXt modernizes a ResNet into a Transformer-like block. It uses a 7×7 depthwise conv for spatial mixing, then a single LayerNorm, then a 1×1 MLP that expands the width 4× with one GELU, then layer scale, then the residual add.

$$F(x)=W_2\,\mathrm{GELU}\!\big(W_1\,\mathrm{LN}(\mathrm{DWConv}_{7\times7}(x))\big),\qquad W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{4C\times C},\; W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times 4C}$$
$$\mathrm{DWConv}(x)_{c,h,w}=\sum_{\Delta h,\Delta w=-3}^{3}K_{c,\Delta h,\Delta w}\,x_{c,h+\Delta h,w+\Delta w}\qquad(\text{one }7\times7\text{ filter per channel; no channel mixing})$$
$$y = x+\frac{b}{1-p}\,\gamma\odot F(x),\qquad \gamma_{\text{init}}=10^{-6}\mathbf{1},\quad b\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-p)\ \text{(stochastic depth; } b\equiv 1-p \text{ at test, factor folded)}$$

The paper's ablation path on ImageNet-1K top-1 (ResNet-50 regime): 76.1 (ResNet-50) → 78.8 (modern recipe alone) → 79.4 (stage ratio 3:3:9:3) → 79.5 (4×4 stride-4 "patchify" stem) → 80.5 (depthwise conv, width 96) → 80.6 (inverted bottleneck) → 79.9 (move depthwise conv up) → 80.6 (7×7 kernel) → 80.6 (ReLU→GELU) → 81.3 (one activation per block) → 81.4 (one norm per block) → 81.5 (BN→LN) → 82.0 (separate 2×2 stride-2 downsampling with LN). Stage widths for ConvNeXt-T are $C=(96,192,384,768)$ with block counts $B=(3,3,9,3)$. The essay's models (92k, 7.06M and 28.6M parameters; the large one has 18 blocks) are custom sizes. The essay does not give their widths or depths beyond these numbers.

Source: Liu et al. 2022, "A ConvNet for the 2020s", arXiv:2201.03545 (Sec. 2, Fig. 2/4, Table 5); essay "Exploring Image Models".

6.1.3 Normalization: LRN (InceptionV1) vs BatchNorm vs LayerNorm

InceptionV1 (GoogLeNet) normalizes with local response normalization in its stem, which divides each channel by the energy of its neighboring channels. BatchNorm normalizes each channel with batch statistics. ConvNeXt's LayerNorm normalizes each spatial position across its channels.

$$\textbf{LRN:}\quad b_{c}=a_{c}\Big/\Big(k+\tfrac{\alpha}{n}\!\!\sum_{c'=\max(0,c-n/2)}^{\min(C-1,c+n/2)}\!\! a_{c'}^{2}\Big)^{\beta}\qquad(\text{AlexNet: }k=2,\;n=5,\;\alpha=10^{-4},\;\beta=0.75)$$
$$\textbf{BN:}\quad \hat x_{n,c,h,w}=g_c\,\frac{x_{n,c,h,w}-\mu^{\mathcal B}_c}{\sqrt{(\sigma^{\mathcal B}_c)^2+\epsilon}}+b_c,\qquad \mu^{\mathcal B}_c=\tfrac{1}{NHW}\textstyle\sum_{n,h,w}x_{n,c,h,w}$$
$$\textbf{LN (channels-last, per position):}\quad \hat x_{c}=g_c\,\frac{x_{c}-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}+b_c,\qquad \mu=\tfrac1C\textstyle\sum_c x_c,\;\; \sigma^2=\tfrac1C\textstyle\sum_c (x_c-\mu)^2$$

The essay found that ConvNeXt puts almost all of its residual activation mass into one unread coordinate, which "it can change to scale all the other dimensions that are read through LayerNorm". At medium ConvNeXt stage 3 block 8, coordinate 112 holds "94.5% of the raw energy", and the figure's gain is $1/\sqrt{\text{channel variance}+10^{-6}}$. Why one coordinate works as a global volume knob: if $x_k=s$ is large and the others are $O(1)$, then $\mu\approx s/C$ and $\sigma^2\approx s^2(C-1)/C^2\approx s^2/C$. The LN gain on every other channel is then $\approx\sqrt{C}/|s|$. Writing $s$ sets the read-out scale of the whole position (the essay calls this a "saliency signal"), and $x_k$ need not be read by any neuron.

$$\text{gain}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2(x)+\epsilon}}\;\approx\;\frac{\sqrt{C}}{|x_k|}\quad\text{when } x_k^2\gg\textstyle\sum_{c\ne k}x_c^2$$

Source: Szegedy et al. 2015 (GoogLeNet); Krizhevsky et al. 2012 (LRN); Ioffe and Szegedy 2015; Ba et al. 2016; essay sections "The Residual Stream" and "Looking at the Residual Stream" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).

6.1.4 GELU, ReLU, and the "votes" picture of a neuron

The essay describes a neuron as a sum of signed votes before its nonlinearity. In a ReLU network, its negative side gets no gradient directly, but each vote does whenever the neuron is on.

$$z_j=\sum_i w_{ji}a_i+b_j,\qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial w_{ji}}=\delta_j\,\phi'(z_j)\,a_i,\qquad \phi=\mathrm{ReLU}\Rightarrow \phi'(z_j)=\mathbf 1[z_j\gt 0]$$
$$\mathrm{GELU}(z)=z\,\Phi(z)\approx \tfrac12 z\Big(1+\tanh\!\big(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,(z+0.044715z^3)\big)\Big),\qquad \min_z \mathrm{GELU}(z)\approx-0.17\ \text{at } z\approx-0.75$$

Quote: "A neuron is built from votes: each input weight votes for its positive side or its negative side, and the votes add up before the nonlinearity. The negative side never gets gradient on its own, but the individual votes do whenever the neuron is on". Two of our observations. (i) GELU's derivative $\Phi(z)+z\varphi(z)$ is nonzero for $z\lt 0$, so its negative side gets a small direct gradient. (ii) The essay reports negative-pole values such as −61 and −37 in ConvNeXt. These are far below GELU's floor of −0.17, so its poles must be measured on a signed quantity (pre-activation or residual-branch output channel), not on the post-GELU hidden unit. The essay does not say which tensor it calls a ConvNeXt "neuron".

Source: Hendrycks and Gimpel 2016 (GELU); essay "How I Think About Neurons" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).

6.2 Training-recipe regularization

The essay attributes the 50%→70% step to retraining InceptionV1 "with more reasonable modern regularization" but does not list the regularizers. It also says the ConvNeXt comparison used the "same data and recipe". The standard modern recipe is the one in the ConvNeXt paper. Assigning these specific terms to the essay's 70% retrain is our assumption.

$$\textbf{AdamW:}\quad m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t,\quad v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2,\quad \theta_t=\theta_{t-1}-\eta_t\Big(\frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon}+\lambda\,\theta_{t-1}\Big)$$

The decay $\lambda\theta$ is applied outside the adaptive preconditioner ("decoupled"). With L2-in-the-loss Adam, the penalty gradient is divided by $\sqrt{\hat v}$, so it shrinks least where gradients are large. ConvNeXt uses $\eta=4\times10^{-3}$ (batch 4096, 20-epoch linear warmup, cosine decay, 300 epochs), $\lambda=0.05$ and $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$.

$$\textbf{Label smoothing:}\quad q_k=(1-\varepsilon)\,\mathbf 1[k=y]+\frac{\varepsilon}{K},\qquad \mathcal L=-\sum_k q_k\log p_k,\qquad \varepsilon=0.1$$
$$\textbf{Mixup:}\quad \tilde x=\lambda x_i+(1-\lambda)x_j,\;\; \tilde y=\lambda y_i+(1-\lambda)y_j,\;\; \lambda\sim\mathrm{Beta}(0.8,0.8)$$
$$\textbf{CutMix:}\quad \tilde x=M\odot x_i+(\mathbf 1-M)\odot x_j,\;\; \tilde y=\lambda y_i+(1-\lambda)y_j,\;\; \lambda=1-\tfrac{|\text{box}|}{HW},\; \lambda_0\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$$
$$\textbf{EMA of weights:}\quad \bar\theta_t=\tau\,\bar\theta_{t-1}+(1-\tau)\,\theta_t,\qquad \tau=0.9999\ \ (\text{evaluate }\bar\theta)$$

Other settings: RandAugment (9, 0.5), Random Erasing 0.25, stochastic depth 0.1/0.4/0.5/0.5 for T/S/B/L, layer scale $10^{-6}$, no gradient clipping. One hypothesis for why these help neuron legibility: label smoothing and mixup bound the logit margins and reward behavior that is linear between classes. That penalizes very high-gain, idiosyncratic units, and those are a channel for "bad learning", one of the essay's five causes of apparent polysemanticity. The essay does not test which term matters.

Source: Loshchilov and Hutter 2019 (AdamW); Szegedy et al. 2016 (label smoothing); Zhang et al. 2018 (mixup); Yun et al. 2019 (CutMix); Liu et al. 2022 arXiv:2201.03545 Table 5; essay (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).

6.3 Feature visualization

6.3.1 Objective with transformation robustness

Feature visualization optimizes an input to drive a unit. The 2017 recipe has three parts: optimize parameters $\theta$ of a decorrelated image parameterization $P$, take the expectation over small random transformations, and optionally add regularizers.

$$\theta^\star=\arg\max_\theta\;\mathbb E_{t\sim\mathcal T}\Big[\,\mathcal O\big(f(t(P(\theta)))\big)\Big]-\sum_r\lambda_r\,R_r\big(P(\theta)\big),\qquad x^\star=P(\theta^\star)$$
$$\mathcal O_{\text{neuron}}=z_{\ell}[h,w,j],\qquad \mathcal O_{\text{channel}}=\sum_{h,w}z_\ell[h,w,j],\qquad \mathcal O_{\text{class}}=\ell_k\ (\text{pre-softmax logit})$$

The transformation distribution in the Distill article is: pad 16 px, jitter up to 16 px, scale $\in\{1,0.975,1.025,0.95,1.05\}$, rotate $\in\{-5,\dots,5\}^\circ$, jitter up to 8 px, then crop. The defaults were 2560 steps of Adam at learning rate 0.05. Lucid's standard_transforms are: pad 12 (constant 0.5), jitter 8, scale $1+(i-5)/50$ for $i=0..10$, rotate $-10..10^\circ$ (extra weight on $0^\circ$), jitter 4. The essay: "It turned out the method was missing transformation robustness. Once researchers added it, the same neurons came out crisp."

Source: Olah, Mordvintsev, Schubert 2017, https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/; lucid/optvis/transform.py (github.com/tensorflow/lucid); essay "A History of Microscope Errors".

6.3.2 Decorrelated (Fourier + color) parameterization

Optimizing raw pixels favors high frequencies. Lucid instead optimizes Fourier coefficients scaled by $1/f$, so all frequencies start with equal energy. It then maps a decorrelated color space to RGB with the square root of ImageNet's color covariance, and applies a sigmoid.

$$P(\theta)=\sigma\!\Big(\tilde C\;\tfrac14\,\mathcal F^{-1}_{2D}\big[S\odot(\theta_{\Re}+i\,\theta_{\Im})\big]\Big),\qquad S(f)=\frac{\sqrt{WH}}{\max\!\big(|f|,\,1/\max(W,H)\big)^{d}},\;\; d=1$$
$$\tilde C=\frac{C_{\sqrt{}}}{\max_k\|C_{\sqrt{},:k}\|_2},\qquad C_{\sqrt{}}=\begin{pmatrix}0.26\;\;0.09\;\;0.02\\0.27\;\;0.00\;\;-0.05\\0.27\;\;-0.09\;\;0.03\end{pmatrix}\;(\text{SVD square root of ImageNet RGB correlation})$$

The article describes this as a preconditioner. Gradient descent in a whitened basis amounts to steepest descent under a metric that does not over-reward high frequencies ("a good first guess is one that makes your data decorrelated and whitened").

Source: lucid/optvis/param/spatial.py (fft_image, rfft2d_freqs) and param/color.py (color_correlation_svd_sqrt), github.com/tensorflow/lucid; https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/ ("Preconditioning and Parameterization").

6.3.3 Regularizers

$$R_{\text{TV}}(x)=\sum_{h,w,c}\big|x_{h+1,w,c}-x_{h,w,c}\big|+\big|x_{h,w+1,c}-x_{h,w,c}\big|,\qquad R_{L1}(x)=\sum|x-c_0|$$
$$R_{\text{blur}}(x)=\tfrac12\big\|x-\mathrm{sg}[\mathrm{blur}(x)]\big\|_2^2\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; x\leftarrow(1-k)\,x+k\,\mathrm{blur}(x)\ \text{per step}$$

6.3.5 Failure mode: reward hacking in deep layers, and the path-anchored fix

In late InceptionV1 layers, free optimization finds routes that natural images rarely use. "It takes the easiest path, which in InceptionV1 often means dogs ... the early optimization falls into them and never recovers." The fix is to bias early optimization toward the routes that real top examples use: "We took the neuron's top dataset examples, traced the paths they take, and gave the optimization an incentive to follow those paths, often only for the first 64 steps". The essay says "we don't have a canonical best one". It notes elsewhere that "the path weight is scaled to each unit's activation range".

$$\nabla_x a_j=\phi'(z_j)\sum_i w_{ji}\,\nabla_x a_i\qquad(\text{the ascent direction is dominated by upstream units with large } w_{ji}\,\|\nabla_x a_i\|)$$
$$\text{path-anchored:}\quad \mathcal J_s(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\big[a_j(x_\theta)\big]+\lambda_s\,\rho_j\,\cos\!\big(A_{\cdot\to j}(x_\theta),\,\bar r_j\big),\qquad \bar r_j=\tfrac1K\sum_{x\in D_j^+(K)}A_{\cdot\to j}(x),\quad \lambda_s=\lambda_0\,\mathbf 1[s\le 64]$$

The first equation is the standard chain rule. The specific path term (cosine to the mean incoming attribution profile $\bar r_j$ of the top-$K$ examples, gated to the first 64 steps and scaled by the unit's activation range $\rho_j$) is our formalization. The essay describes the incentive only qualitatively. Our reading of the "dog basin" is a rich-get-richer ascent: InceptionV1 has many dog units with positive weights into late units, so the first steps amplify them, and the iterate settles in a basin far from the car route.

Source: essay, main text "Cars and Animals" and appendix "Feature Visualization in Later Layers" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).

6.4 Dataset examples, percentiles, poles, and the census

6.4.1 Positive and negative poles

$$s_j(x)=\max_{h,w} z_j(x)[h,w],\;\; s^{\min}_j(x)=\min_{h,w} z_j(x)[h,w],\qquad D_j^{+}(k)=\operatorname*{top\text{-}k}_{x\in\mathcal D}\,s_j(x),\qquad D_j^{-}(k)=\operatorname*{top\text{-}k}_{x\in\mathcal D}\,\big(-s^{\min}_j(x)\big)$$

$|\mathcal D|=50{,}000$ images in the essay's vision scans ("Each neuron's 20 strongest responses out of 50,000 images"; "Photos rank each neuron's image maxima over 50,000 training images"). The negative pole is the argmin set. The essay reads it as a gate that names the distinction being drawn ("the V neuron's negative side is an O"; "+ Taxis − Ambulances"). For ReLU nets, poles are read on pre-activations. Curve Detectors: "When studying ReLU networks, we find it helpful to look at the distribution of pre-activation values." For SwiGLU, see §8.

Source: essay "How I Think About Neurons"; https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/curve-detectors/ ("Dataset Analysis").

6.4.2 Percentiles vs raw activations

$$q_j(x)=\hat F_j\big(a_j(x)\big)=\frac1N\sum_{x'}\mathbf 1\big[a_j(x')\le a_j(x)\big],\qquad r_j(x)=\frac{a_j(x)}{\max_{x'}a_j(x')}$$
$$\text{mean spacing between events above percentile } p:\;\; \frac{1}{1-p}\ \text{tokens}\;\Rightarrow\; p=0.99\!:\,100,\quad 0.999\!:\,10^3,\quad 0.9999\!:\,10^4$$

At about 400 tokens per page, these spacings match the essay's "1 token in 100 is about a quarter of a page ... the 99.9th percentile is every two or three pages, the 99.99th every 25 or 30". The essay prefers the raw shape $r_j$. "Percentiles flatten that, and it's often what tells you whether you're looking at a dial, where the middle matters, or a detector, where it mostly doesn't." For example, the Christmas neuron drops off "at about 5% of its max". Tails are exponential, not Gaussian: $p(z)\propto e^{-z/s}$ (Curve Detectors, crediting Brice Ménard). Curve Detectors weights the activation spectrum by contribution to expected value, $x\,p(x)$, or by $x\,\tfrac{d\,\text{logit}}{dx}\,p(x)$. Multimodal Neurons, which Cammarata co-authored, credits him with discovering "that negative pre-ReLU activations are often interpretable" and "that neurons sometimes contain a distinct regime change between medium and strong activations". Both are early forms of the essay's negative-pole and raw-shape views.

Source: essay "Percentiles or raw activations?"; https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/curve-detectors/; https://distill.pub/2021/multimodal-neurons/.

6.4.3 The "one main job" census

The essay's headline census, for Qwen3-32B, is per neuron pole: "roughly 90% of neuron poles have one main job, often with small related offshoots; another ~7% have two related jobs; and only ~2-4% are not yet understood". Restricted to strong activations, it is "closer to 97% non-arbitrary mixtures" (small samples). The essay says the number depends on "the threshold for counting weak activations, depth, density of sub-circuits, and what you view as 'one thing'", and gives no formula.

$$S_{j,\pm}(\tau)=\Big\{x:\ \pm a_j(x)\ \ge\ \tau\cdot\max_{x'}\big(\pm a_j(x')\big)\Big\},\qquad S_{j,\pm}(\tau)=\textstyle\bigsqcup_{m=1}^{M}J_m,\quad \pi_m=\frac{\sum_{x\in J_m}|a_j(x)|}{\sum_{x\in S}|a_j(x)|}$$
$$\text{one main job}\iff \pi_1\ge\theta\ \wedge\ \forall m\gt 1:\ d(J_m,J_1)\le\delta;\qquad \text{two related jobs}\iff M_{\text{major}}=2\ \wedge\ d(J_1,J_2)\le\delta$$

$\tau$, $\theta$, $\delta$, the clustering into jobs $J_m$ and the semantic distance $d$ are our placeholders. In the essay, an agent pipeline and judge do this step, writing 1,000 "neuron papers" on Qwen3-32B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B neurons. The essay's tests of explanations are concrete. Blind maximum: a second agent sees only the explanation and writes an input that should maximize the neuron, and these inputs beat the best of 10B corpus tokens. Near-miss controls: for 200 random neurons, 183 with positive-side explanations, new examples fired strongly 76% of the time versus 11% for near misses (won 164, tied 15, lost 4). Earlier censuses for comparison: Multimodal Neurons, CLIP final conv layer, $n=50$: 76% interpretable (95% CI 64–88%), 18% polysemantic with interpretable facets, 6% uninterpretable. Bills et al. 2023: about 1.7% of GPT-2 XL neurons interpretable. Towards Monosemanticity: median neuron 0/14. GPT-2 XL re-audited: about 70% roughly one thing, about 5% pristine.

Source: essay "The Short Version" footnote 1, "Explanations That Can Lose", "Slouching Towards Monosemanticity"; https://already-clean.goodfire.com/neuron-scientist/papers/; https://distill.pub/2021/multimodal-neurons/ (Guided Tour footnote).

6.5 Attribution, ablation, steering

6.5.1 Gradient × activation and direct weights

$$A_{i\to y}(x)=a_i(x)\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial a_i}(x)\qquad\text{(Building Blocks: activation times gradient)}$$
$$A_{i\to j}(x)=a_i(x)\,\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial a_i}(x)\;\overset{\text{adjacent, linear }z_j}{=}\;W_{ji}\,a_i(x),\qquad \text{conv: }A_{i\to j}=\sum_{\Delta}W_{j,i,\Delta}\,a_i[\cdot+\Delta]$$
$$\text{residual, LN gain frozen:}\quad W^{\text{virt}}_{ji}=\big(W_{\text{in}}^{(j)}\big)^{\!\top}\,\mathrm{diag}(g/\sigma)\,W_{\text{out}}^{(i)}$$

Building Blocks: "we estimate the effect of a neuron on the output is its activation times the rate at which increasing its activation increases the output." Curve Detectors: "The attribution tensor describing how all neurons in the previous layer influenced a given neuron is the activations pointwise multiplied by the weights". The essay reports edges as "mean routed attribution" over 8 examples, with a sign-agreement count (e.g. "+0.335768; 8/8 examples share the mean sign"). Its graph target is "the ground-truth class's one-vs-rest log-odds margin". The virtual-weight form across the residual stream is the standard frozen-LN linearization, not stated in the essay. "RelP", the essay's name for its graph method, is not defined in the text.

$$\bar A_{i\to j}=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{i\to j}(x_k),\qquad \text{agree}_{ij}=\#\{k:\operatorname{sign}A_{i\to j}(x_k)=\operatorname{sign}\bar A_{i\to j}\},\qquad m(x)=\ell_y-\log\sum_{c\ne y}e^{\ell_c}$$

6.5.2 Attribution graphs and edge motifs

$$\mathcal G(x)=(V,E),\quad V=\{j: |a_j(x)|\ \text{large}\},\quad E=\{(i\!\to\! j,\ \bar A_{i\to j}) : |\bar A_{i\to j}|\ge\kappa\};\qquad \mathbf E\in\mathbb R^{n_{\text{graphs}}\times n_{\text{edges}}}\approx UV$$

The essay reports "Factoring 25,000 ImageNet attribution graphs gave us connected circuits of ordinary neurons", for example a teapot motif. 6.5.3 Ablation, weak-activation removal, steering

$$\textbf{ablate:}\ h_i\leftarrow 0,\qquad \textbf{effect: }\Delta P=P(y\mid \mathrm{do}(h_S=0))-P(y);\qquad \textbf{steer:}\ h_i\leftarrow h_i+\alpha\quad(\text{or } h_i\leftarrow \alpha\,\max h_i)$$
$$\textbf{weak-activation removal:}\ h_i\leftarrow h_i\,\mathbf 1\big[|h_i|\ge\tau_i\big],\ \tau_i=Q_{0.75}(|h_i|)\qquad \textbf{extreme removal:}\ \text{out}_i\leftarrow 0\ \text{if}\ g_i\gt Q^{\text{ordinary}}_{0.999}(g_i)$$

Kimi K3: removing one "holds back" neuron moves P(return) from 0.83 to 0.98. Gate above its 99.9th percentile zeroed: Psalm 23 recitation fails. 6.6 Sparse autoencoders and the near-copy comparison

$$z=\mathrm{TopK}_k\big(W_{\text{enc}}(x-b_{\text{pre}})\big),\qquad \hat x=W_{\text{dec}}z+b_{\text{pre}},\qquad \mathcal L=\|x-\hat x\|_2^2+\alpha\,\|e-\hat e\|_2^2,\;\; \hat e=W_{\text{dec}}\,\mathrm{TopK}_{k_{\text{aux}}}(z_{\text{dead}})$$

The TopK SAE of Gao et al. 2024 has an auxiliary loss that reconstructs the residual $e=x-\hat x$ from dead latents. The essay's crushed-layer SAE: 640 features, $k=2$, auxiliary loss on, 25.6M positions from 200,000 ImageNet images. It reaches 82% held-out variance explained ($\mathrm{FVU}=0.18$), and spliced in, it drops top-1 from 58.8% to 43.0%. Matched to the original curve detectors (24–56° wide, silent at the opposite angle), the best SAE features are 44–174° wide and two-sided, with shape correlation 0.38–0.79. An SAE on the original layer matches at 0.96–1.00.

$$c_f=\max_i\big|\cos(d_f,e_i)\big|,\qquad \text{near-copy}(f)\iff c_f\ge\kappa,\qquad \text{share}=\frac{\sum_{f:\,\text{near-copy}}\mathbb E[z_f]}{\sum_f\mathbb E[z_f]}\;=\;0.88\ (\text{essay, last layer})$$
$$\text{curve-neuron share of SAE feature }f:\ \ \frac{\sum_{i\in\text{curve}}|d_{f,i}|}{\sum_i|d_{f,i}|}\in\{97.5\%,98.0\%,97.2\%\}\ (\text{essay examples})$$
$$\text{rotation search:}\quad R^\star=\arg\max_{R\in SO(n)}\ \mathbb E_x\Big[\textstyle\sum_i (R\,h(x))_i^4\Big]\ \Rightarrow\ R^\star\approx\text{signed permutation (the neuron basis)}$$

6.7 Superposition vs recursive generalization

6.7.1 The toy model of superposition

$$h=Wx,\qquad x'=\mathrm{ReLU}(W^{\top}h+b)=\mathrm{ReLU}(W^{\top}Wx+b),\qquad \mathcal L=\sum_x\sum_i I_i\,(x_i-x'_i)^2$$
$$x_i=0\ \text{w.p. } S_i,\ \text{else } x_i\sim U[0,1];\qquad D_i=\frac{\|W_i\|^2}{\sum_j(\hat W_i\cdot W_j)^2}\qquad(\text{interference: off-diagonal } (W^\top W)_{ij})$$

The essay's critique: the toy "prices the cost of sharing a neuron, interference, but not the benefit, reuse", because "every feature is an end in itself, and nothing downstream builds on what two features share". The essay's modified toy has 20 inputs in 4 correlated groups of 5, a 5-dimensional bottleneck, and an added layer of hidden neurons. In it, "related inputs grew onto the neurons". The share of bottleneck weight along the data's top principal components was 0.37 (original toy, density 0.1), 0.80 (40 hidden neurons, density 0.1) and 0.79 (original toy, dense 0.9, PCA-like).

$$\text{PC share}(W)=\frac{\|W P_k\|_F^2}{\|W\|_F^2},\qquad P_k=\text{projector onto top-}k\text{ principal components of }\mathrm{Cov}(x)$$

The PC-share formula is our reading of "share of the bottleneck's weights along the data's top principal components".

Source: Elhage et al. 2022, https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html; essay appendix "Toy Models".

6.7.2 Two opposite predictions

The essay frames generalization and superposition as making opposite, testable predictions: "generalization should be local, superposition should be non-local". "Generalization would want a floppy ear dog neuron ... Superposition would want a floppy ear dog + Latin poetry neuron." With a semantic distance $d$ and co-occurrence probability $P(u\wedge v)$ over the members of a neuron's receptive set $S_j$:

$$\textbf{generalization:}\ \ \mathbb E_{u,v\in S_j}\,d(u,v)\ \text{small};\qquad \textbf{superposition:}\ \ S_j=\arg\min_{S}\sum_{u\ne v\in S}P(u\wedge v)\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \mathbb E_{u,v\in S_j}\,d(u,v)\ \text{large}$$

Source: essay "Superposition" and "Superposition of the Gaps" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).

6.7.3 Recursive generalization (our formalization)

The essay's definition: "a neuron generalizing along more than one axis, often first along one axis, then sprouting outwards in ways that are easy to follow locally, but stop it from being 'monosemantic' in the end". Example: "iy" → "diy" → "do it yourself". Let each input $u$ have a set of facets $\phi(u)$ (sub-features such as the letters "iy", the meaning DIY, yellow, field). Neuron $j$'s receptive set at training time $t$ is $S_j(t)=\{u: a_j^{(t)}(u)\ge\tau\}$. Recursive generalization is the growth rule below.

$$u'\in S_j(t+1)\setminus S_j(t)\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists\,u\in S_j(t):\ \phi(u)\cap\phi(u')\ne\varnothing$$

So the final set $S_j(T)$ is path-connected under the facet-sharing graph and rooted at the seed $S_j(t_0)$. Its endpoints need not share any facet: $\phi(\text{"iy"})\cap\phi(\text{"do it yourself"})=\varnothing$ while each adjacent pair overlaps. Single-concept monosemanticity fails, but the set has a narrow, checkable shape in both semantic space and time. That is the essay's "specific shape in both semantic space and time, and a narrow target to hit by chance". The mechanism the essay proposes ("A neuron that fires on 'iy' is already on for 'diy,' so it's first in line") follows from the ReLU gradient in §1.4. On a new input $u'$, only neurons with $z_j(u')\gt 0$ receive weight updates, and the update is proportional to their current activation of shared inputs.

$$\Delta w_j\ \propto\ -\,\delta_j(u')\,\mathbf 1[z_j(u')\gt 0]\,a_{\text{in}}(u')\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{neurons already partly on for }u'\text{ (shared facet) are recruited first}$$

A linear model of the essay's "circuit race" (the Labrador example). Two circuits, broad ($b$) and exact ($e$), share the residual error and receive gradient in proportion to their coverage $c_b\gg c_e$ (fraction of the input they fire on):

$$\epsilon(t)=y-u_b(t)-u_e(t),\qquad \dot u_k=\eta\,c_k\,\epsilon(t)\ \Rightarrow\ \epsilon(t)=y\,e^{-\eta(c_b+c_e)t},\qquad u_k(\infty)=\frac{c_k}{c_b+c_e}\,y$$

The broad circuit captures a fraction $c_b/(c_b+c_e)$ of the target, and the exact circuit "starves" because the shared error vanishes. On the next example the broad circuit starts partly on, which is the rich-get-richer step. This matches coherent gradients (Chatterjee 2020): per-example gradients $g=\sum_x g_x$ add along shared directions. The essay's whole-network version: a blank 7.1M model alone reaches 48–50% on Flowers-102, but trained jointly with a pretrained twin on a shared objective it reaches about 2%, while the twin takes 92.8–97.4% of the "marginal objective credit".

Source: essay "Recursive Generalization", appendices "Why Networks Generalize", "Circuit Racing", "More on Circuit Ecology", "Is Recursive Generalization Hard to Vary?"; Multimodal Neurons "conjoined features" (https://distill.pub/2021/multimodal-neurons/: concepts "overlapping in a superficial way in one facet, and then generalizing out in multiple directions").

6.7.4 Measuring it: watching neurons over training

$$\text{share}_{j,g}(t)=\frac{\big|D^{+}_j(k;\theta_t)\cap G_g\big|}{k},\qquad \text{locality}(t)=\min_{u\in D_j^+(k;\theta_{t-1})} d\big(u_{\text{new}},u\big)\ \ \text{vs}\ \ d\big(u_{\text{new}},\bar u_{\text{seed}}\big)$$

The essay tracks the same coordinate across checkpoints of a retrained network. For InceptionV1 neuron 338 at epochs 0.7, 1.5, 3 and 12, image groups $G_g$ shift from rapeseed fields and valleys toward tennis balls, terriers and broccoli. ConvNeXt neuron 399 stays rapeseed. InceptionV1 mixed5a:178 is tracked at steps 0 to 30,000. The plotted "grey band behind each row is its share of the neuron's strongest images", which is $\text{share}_{j,g}(t)$. The locality statistic is our proposal. Recursive generalization predicts that new members are near some existing member even when they are far from the seed's centroid. Superposition predicts new members far from all existing members. The squeeze experiments are the static counterpart. With mixed3b cut from 480 to 10 channels (48×) and no bypass, the neurons become "coarser" ("one 'curves & loops' neuron instead of ten curve detectors"), not mixed: "Fewer neurons make generalizing neurons coarser, never fractured."

Source: essay "Recursive Generalization" figure, appendix "Watching Neurons Develop", "What Neurons Do Under Pressure".

6.8 SwiGLU and the double-negative regime

$$h=\mathrm{SiLU}(W_g x)\odot(W_v x),\qquad \text{out}=W_o h,\qquad a=\mathrm{SiLU}(g)\cdot v,\qquad \mathrm{SiLU}(g)=g\,\sigma(g)\ge -0.278$$
$$a=\underbrace{a\,\mathbf 1[g\gt 0,v\gt 0]}_{(+,+)}+\underbrace{a\,\mathbf 1[g\lt 0,v\lt 0]}_{(-,-)\ \text{double negative}}+\underbrace{a\,\mathbf 1[g\gt 0,v\lt 0]}_{(+,-)}+\underbrace{a\,\mathbf 1[g\lt 0,v\gt 0]}_{(-,+)},\qquad (-,-):\ 0\lt a\le 0.278\,|v|$$

The essay: "When both are negative, the output is positive, so a neuron can fire for two quite different reasons." "A negative gate leaves a small negative factor. Multiplying it by a negative value produces a positive activation." The double-negative "echo" "usually look[s] like the negative pole". "Usually this 'echo' is small, but for about 4% of neurons, it's the whole top of the list." Ablating these across the whole network "ups most benchmarks, but lowers some reasoning ones". In the Qwen3-32B L1/N20736 example, every top-list firing has the gate closed, "a small sign-flipped leak of the value, under 8% of the neuron's peak". With the leak separated, the neuron's job is "the token that completes 'Russia' or 'Russian,' in any script". In vision SwiGLU, "the value tends to learn broader things, and gate more specific things". The bound $a\le 0.278|v|$ in the $(-,-)$ regime follows from $\min\mathrm{SiLU}=-0.278$ (at $g\approx-1.28$). Why it fools top-$k$ dashboards: when $|v|$ is large on the value's negative side, $0.278|v|$ can exceed the $(+,+)$ responses of a sparse, narrow gate. The fix the essay uses is to report poles per regime, $D^{+}_{j}$ restricted to $\mathbf 1[g\gt 0]$.

Source: Shazeer 2020 (GLU variants); essay "Exploring Language Models" (SwiGLU paragraph), appendix "Separating the Sign Regimes" (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B L36/E206/N103, 750 quantile-stratified examples, 626 retained routing) (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).

6.8.1 Mixture-of-experts as "anti-superposition" (essay argument)

$$p(e\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}(W_r x)_e,\qquad \text{neuron } j\in e\ \text{fires only if } e\in\operatorname{TopK}_e\,p(e\mid x)$$

The essay argues that "the router would have to recognize all ten thousand to know when to send a token there, and the router is just one small linear layer". Formally, an expert receives the token set $\{x: w_e^\top x\ge \max_{e'\ne e}w_{e'}^\top x\}$ (for top-1), which is a convex polyhedral cone. Packing many unrelated, semantically scattered items into one expert requires them to lie in one such cone, while the related items that are not packed must lie outside it. A linear router makes that unlikely.

Source: essay "Exploring Language Models" (expert specialization paragraph).
