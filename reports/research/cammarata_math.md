### Key facts in the essay worth carrying into the report
These go beyond the essay's headline summary.
1. **Interpretability ladder for InceptionV1's last layer:** about 5% (2017 tools) → about 50% (fixed FV and attribution, same 2014 weights) → about 70% (retrained with modern regularization) → about 99% (parameter-matched 7.06M ConvNeXt). The essay's three ConvNeXts have 92k, 7.06M and 28.6M parameters (the large one has 18 blocks). The essay names the architecture changes only as "residual connections, LayerNorm, and a couple other improvements". **It never specifies the regularization recipe.**
2. **Census:** for Qwen3-32B, about 90% of neuron *poles* have one main job, about 7% two related jobs, and about 2–4% are not understood. On strong activations it is about 97% non-arbitrary. Dense and MoE models look similar, with similar results up to Kimi K3 (trillions of parameters). The essay presents the first published neuron-only attribution graph of a multi-trillion-parameter model.
3. **Feature-visualization reward hacking:** free optimization in late InceptionV1 falls into a "dog basin". The fix anchors optimization to the attribution paths of top dataset examples "often only for the first 64 steps", with the path weight scaled to each unit's activation range. Under guided optimization, two of the car neuron 4c:447's readers turn out to be car neurons and two are shared car/dog *pose* neurons. InceptionV1 has "more than a hundred" generalized pose neurons.
4. **Squeeze tests:** mixed3b cut from 480 to 10 channels with no bypass (58.35% / 81.14% top-1/top-5). The neurons became coarser ("curves & loops"), not mixed. A TopK SAE (640 features, k=2, 82% variance explained) on the squeezed layer gives broad, two-sided curve features (44–174° wide, shape correlation 0.38–0.79), against 0.96–1.00 for an SAE on the original layer. Crushing the medium ConvNeXt's residual stream to 46 dimensions drops accuracy from 72% to 60%/39%, but "Nothing looks cleverly interleaved".
5. **SAE vs neurons:** 88% of last-layer SAE activity is near-copies of single neurons. Removing non-matching dictionary directions leaves accuracy unchanged. A rotation search for concentrated activity returns to the neuron basis "in every block". The Missing-Curves SAE features are 97–98% composed of existing curve neurons.
6. **Weak activations:** removing the weakest 75% gives 72%→71%; removing the strongest 7% (equal total activation) gives 72%→4%. For InceptionV1, keeping about the top quarter of positive activations gives 69.47% vs 70.08%.
7. **Residual stream "sink":** in ConvNeXt, coordinate 112 holds 94.5% of raw energy and acts with LayerNorm as a global gain knob. Removing it from the projection makes the residual-stream maps legible.
8. **SwiGLU double negative:** $a=\mathrm{SiLU}(g)\,v$ is positive for $g\lt0, v\lt0$. For about 4% of neurons this "echo" fills the top of the activation list. Ablating these neurons network-wide "ups most benchmarks, but lowers some reasoning ones". In vision SwiGLU, the value is broad and the gate specific.
9. **Learning dynamics:** neurons were watched over epochs 0.7–12 (InceptionV1 n338 vs ConvNeXt n399) and over steps 0–30k (mixed5a:178). Growth is local. "Circuit racing": a blank model trained alongside a pretrained twin on a shared objective learns about 2% vs 48–50% alone. Memorization (umbrella→bee in 9 steps) reuses existing bee neurons in the last 3 blocks.
10. **Explanation tests:** blind maximum, where agent-written inputs beat the best of 10B corpus tokens. Near-miss controls on 200 random neurons: 76% vs 11% strong firing; explanations won 164, tied 15, lost 4. Edge motifs from factoring 25,000 ImageNet attribution graphs predict where ablation hurts.

<h3>0. Notation and the interpretability ladder</h3>
<p>Write $x$ for an input (image or token context), $z_j(x)$ for the pre-activation of neuron $j$, and $a_j(x)=\phi(z_j(x))$ for its activation. For convolutional channels, a per-image score is the spatial maximum. The essay gives four estimates for the share of interpretable neurons in InceptionV1's last layer. Read as a sequence, they split the gain into tools, training recipe and architecture.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\underbrace{\sim 5\%}_{\text{2014 weights, 2017 tools}} \;\xrightarrow{\;\Delta_{\text{tools}}\approx +45\;}\; \underbrace{\sim 50\%}_{\text{same weights, fixed feature vis + attribution}} \;\xrightarrow{\;\Delta_{\text{recipe}}\approx +20\;}\; \underbrace{\sim 70\%}_{\text{retrained, modern regularization}} \;\xrightarrow{\;\Delta_{\text{arch}}\approx +29\;}\; \underbrace{\sim 99\%}_{\text{ConvNeXt, 7.06M params}}$$</div>
<p><span class="hyp">The additive split is our reading. The essay gives only the four levels, and the steps are not shown to be independent or to commute.</span> The essay's own wording: "I put it around 5% with tools of that era, others at ~0%. With better interpretability tools (fixed feature visualization, methods for visualizing attribution etc) it's more like 50% on the same network ... Retraining the same architecture with more reasonable modern regularization it's more like 70%. In the ConvNeXt of the same size, it's about 99%."</p>
<p class="src">Source: Cammarata 2026, "Neural Networks Are Already Clean", https://already-clean.goodfire.com/ (section "A History of Microscope Errors").</p>

<h3>1. Architecture</h3>

<h4>1.1 Residual stream: blocks write deltas</h4>
<p>InceptionV1 is a plain feed-forward stack. Each layer's output is the only path forward, so its channels must carry everything later layers need. A residual network adds each block's output to a running stream.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{plain (InceptionV1):}\quad x_{\ell+1}=F_\ell(x_\ell) \qquad\qquad \text{residual (ConvNeXt):}\quad x_{\ell+1}=x_\ell+\gamma_\ell\odot F_\ell(x_\ell)$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$x_L = x_0+\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\gamma_\ell\odot F_\ell(x_\ell),\qquad \frac{\partial x_L}{\partial x_\ell}= I+\sum_{m=\ell}^{L-1}\frac{\partial\,(\gamma_m\odot F_m(x_m))}{\partial x_\ell}$$</div>
<p>The identity term gives every block a direct gradient path and a direct read path to the output. <span class="hyp">The "delta" argument, as we formalize it: in a plain net, layer $\ell$'s $C_\ell$ channels are a bottleneck. By the data-processing inequality, $I(x_{\ell+1};y)\le I(a_\ell;y)$, so every downstream-relevant distinction must be re-encoded in $a_\ell$, and neurons must relay information they do not themselves compute. In a residual net, the block's hidden units only need to encode the update $F_\ell(x_\ell)$, while unchanged information passes through on the identity. That removes the relay pressure the essay calls "overflow".</span> The essay states the empirical side. The residual stream "was introduced to help learn deeper neural networks, and it also seems to help the deeper layers be conceptually clean". "Architectural problems made later layers overflow." Its compression tests deliberately remove the bypass: "crush the last layer, stop it from routing around the residual stream".</p>
<p class="src">Source: https://already-clean.goodfire.com/ ("The Short Version"; "What Neurons Do Under Pressure"); He et al. 2016 (ResNet).</p>

<h4>1.2 The ConvNeXt block (depthwise conv, inverted bottleneck, GELU, layer scale)</h4>
<p>ConvNeXt modernizes a ResNet into a Transformer-like block. It uses a 7×7 depthwise conv for spatial mixing, then a single LayerNorm, then a 1×1 MLP that expands the width 4× with one GELU, then layer scale, then the residual add.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$F(x)=W_2\,\mathrm{GELU}\!\big(W_1\,\mathrm{LN}(\mathrm{DWConv}_{7\times7}(x))\big),\qquad W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{4C\times C},\; W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times 4C}$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\mathrm{DWConv}(x)_{c,h,w}=\sum_{\Delta h,\Delta w=-3}^{3}K_{c,\Delta h,\Delta w}\,x_{c,h+\Delta h,w+\Delta w}\qquad(\text{one }7\times7\text{ filter per channel; no channel mixing})$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$y = x+\frac{b}{1-p}\,\gamma\odot F(x),\qquad \gamma_{\text{init}}=10^{-6}\mathbf{1},\quad b\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-p)\ \text{(stochastic depth; } b\equiv 1-p \text{ at test, factor folded)}$$</div>
<p>The paper's ablation path on ImageNet-1K top-1 (ResNet-50 regime): 76.1 (ResNet-50) → 78.8 (modern recipe alone) → 79.4 (stage ratio 3:3:9:3) → 79.5 (4×4 stride-4 "patchify" stem) → 80.5 (depthwise conv, width 96) → 80.6 (inverted bottleneck) → 79.9 (move depthwise conv up) → 80.6 (7×7 kernel) → 80.6 (ReLU→GELU) → 81.3 (one activation per block) → 81.4 (one norm per block) → 81.5 (BN→LN) → 82.0 (separate 2×2 stride-2 downsampling with LN). Stage widths for ConvNeXt-T are $C=(96,192,384,768)$ with block counts $B=(3,3,9,3)$. <span class="hyp">The essay's models (92k, 7.06M and 28.6M parameters; the large one has 18 blocks) are custom sizes. The essay does not give their widths or depths beyond these numbers.</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: Liu et al. 2022, "A ConvNet for the 2020s", arXiv:2201.03545 (Sec. 2, Fig. 2/4, Table 5); essay "Exploring Image Models".</p>

<h4>1.3 Normalization: LRN (InceptionV1) vs BatchNorm vs LayerNorm</h4>
<p>InceptionV1 (GoogLeNet) normalizes with local response normalization in its stem, which divides each channel by the energy of its neighboring channels. BatchNorm normalizes each channel with batch statistics. ConvNeXt's LayerNorm normalizes each spatial position across its channels.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{LRN:}\quad b_{c}=a_{c}\Big/\Big(k+\tfrac{\alpha}{n}\!\!\sum_{c'=\max(0,c-n/2)}^{\min(C-1,c+n/2)}\!\! a_{c'}^{2}\Big)^{\beta}\qquad(\text{AlexNet: }k=2,\;n=5,\;\alpha=10^{-4},\;\beta=0.75)$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{BN:}\quad \hat x_{n,c,h,w}=g_c\,\frac{x_{n,c,h,w}-\mu^{\mathcal B}_c}{\sqrt{(\sigma^{\mathcal B}_c)^2+\epsilon}}+b_c,\qquad \mu^{\mathcal B}_c=\tfrac{1}{NHW}\textstyle\sum_{n,h,w}x_{n,c,h,w}$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{LN (channels-last, per position):}\quad \hat x_{c}=g_c\,\frac{x_{c}-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}+b_c,\qquad \mu=\tfrac1C\textstyle\sum_c x_c,\;\; \sigma^2=\tfrac1C\textstyle\sum_c (x_c-\mu)^2$$</div>
<p>The essay found that ConvNeXt puts almost all of its residual activation mass into one unread coordinate, which "it can change to scale all the other dimensions that are read through LayerNorm". At medium ConvNeXt stage 3 block 8, coordinate 112 holds "94.5% of the raw energy", and the figure's gain is $1/\sqrt{\text{channel variance}+10^{-6}}$. <span class="hyp">Why one coordinate works as a global volume knob: if $x_k=s$ is large and the others are $O(1)$, then $\mu\approx s/C$ and $\sigma^2\approx s^2(C-1)/C^2\approx s^2/C$. The LN gain on every other channel is then $\approx\sqrt{C}/|s|$. Writing $s$ sets the read-out scale of the whole position (the essay calls this a "saliency signal"), and $x_k$ need not be read by any neuron.</span></p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{gain}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2(x)+\epsilon}}\;\approx\;\frac{\sqrt{C}}{|x_k|}\quad\text{when } x_k^2\gg\textstyle\sum_{c\ne k}x_c^2$$</div>
<p class="src">Source: Szegedy et al. 2015 (GoogLeNet); Krizhevsky et al. 2012 (LRN); Ioffe and Szegedy 2015; Ba et al. 2016; essay sections "The Residual Stream" and "Looking at the Residual Stream" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).</p>

<h4>1.4 GELU, ReLU, and the "votes" picture of a neuron</h4>
<p>The essay describes a neuron as a sum of signed votes before its nonlinearity. In a ReLU network, its negative side gets no gradient directly, but each vote does whenever the neuron is on.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$z_j=\sum_i w_{ji}a_i+b_j,\qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial w_{ji}}=\delta_j\,\phi'(z_j)\,a_i,\qquad \phi=\mathrm{ReLU}\Rightarrow \phi'(z_j)=\mathbf 1[z_j\gt 0]$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\mathrm{GELU}(z)=z\,\Phi(z)\approx \tfrac12 z\Big(1+\tanh\!\big(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,(z+0.044715z^3)\big)\Big),\qquad \min_z \mathrm{GELU}(z)\approx-0.17\ \text{at } z\approx-0.75$$</div>
<p>Quote: "A neuron is built from votes: each input weight votes for its positive side or its negative side, and the votes add up before the nonlinearity. The negative side never gets gradient on its own, but the individual votes do whenever the neuron is on". <span class="hyp">Two of our observations. (i) GELU's derivative $\Phi(z)+z\varphi(z)$ is nonzero for $z\lt 0$, so its negative side gets a small direct gradient. (ii) The essay reports negative-pole values such as −61 and −37 in ConvNeXt. These are far below GELU's floor of −0.17, so its poles must be measured on a signed quantity (pre-activation or residual-branch output channel), not on the post-GELU hidden unit. The essay does not say which tensor it calls a ConvNeXt "neuron".</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: Hendrycks and Gimpel 2016 (GELU); essay "How I Think About Neurons" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).</p>

<h3>2. Training-recipe regularization</h3>
<p>The essay attributes the 50%→70% step to retraining InceptionV1 "with more reasonable modern regularization" but does not list the regularizers. It also says the ConvNeXt comparison used the "same data and recipe". The standard modern recipe is the one in the ConvNeXt paper. <span class="hyp">Assigning these specific terms to the essay's 70% retrain is our assumption.</span></p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{AdamW:}\quad m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t,\quad v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2,\quad \theta_t=\theta_{t-1}-\eta_t\Big(\frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon}+\lambda\,\theta_{t-1}\Big)$$</div>
<p>The decay $\lambda\theta$ is applied outside the adaptive preconditioner ("decoupled"). With L2-in-the-loss Adam, the penalty gradient is divided by $\sqrt{\hat v}$, so it shrinks least where gradients are large. ConvNeXt uses $\eta=4\times10^{-3}$ (batch 4096, 20-epoch linear warmup, cosine decay, 300 epochs), $\lambda=0.05$ and $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{Label smoothing:}\quad q_k=(1-\varepsilon)\,\mathbf 1[k=y]+\frac{\varepsilon}{K},\qquad \mathcal L=-\sum_k q_k\log p_k,\qquad \varepsilon=0.1$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{Mixup:}\quad \tilde x=\lambda x_i+(1-\lambda)x_j,\;\; \tilde y=\lambda y_i+(1-\lambda)y_j,\;\; \lambda\sim\mathrm{Beta}(0.8,0.8)$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{CutMix:}\quad \tilde x=M\odot x_i+(\mathbf 1-M)\odot x_j,\;\; \tilde y=\lambda y_i+(1-\lambda)y_j,\;\; \lambda=1-\tfrac{|\text{box}|}{HW},\; \lambda_0\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{EMA of weights:}\quad \bar\theta_t=\tau\,\bar\theta_{t-1}+(1-\tau)\,\theta_t,\qquad \tau=0.9999\ \ (\text{evaluate }\bar\theta)$$</div>
<p>Other settings: RandAugment (9, 0.5), Random Erasing 0.25, stochastic depth 0.1/0.4/0.5/0.5 for T/S/B/L, layer scale $10^{-6}$, no gradient clipping. <span class="hyp">One hypothesis for why these help neuron legibility: label smoothing and mixup bound the logit margins and reward behavior that is linear between classes. That penalizes very high-gain, idiosyncratic units, and those are a channel for "bad learning", one of the essay's five causes of apparent polysemanticity. The essay does not test which term matters.</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: Loshchilov and Hutter 2019 (AdamW); Szegedy et al. 2016 (label smoothing); Zhang et al. 2018 (mixup); Yun et al. 2019 (CutMix); Liu et al. 2022 arXiv:2201.03545 Table 5; essay (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).</p>

<h3>3. Feature visualization</h3>

<h4>3.1 Objective with transformation robustness</h4>
<p>Feature visualization optimizes an input to drive a unit. The 2017 recipe has three parts: optimize parameters $\theta$ of a decorrelated image parameterization $P$, take the expectation over small random transformations, and optionally add regularizers.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\theta^\star=\arg\max_\theta\;\mathbb E_{t\sim\mathcal T}\Big[\,\mathcal O\big(f(t(P(\theta)))\big)\Big]-\sum_r\lambda_r\,R_r\big(P(\theta)\big),\qquad x^\star=P(\theta^\star)$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\mathcal O_{\text{neuron}}=z_{\ell}[h,w,j],\qquad \mathcal O_{\text{channel}}=\sum_{h,w}z_\ell[h,w,j],\qquad \mathcal O_{\text{class}}=\ell_k\ (\text{pre-softmax logit})$$</div>
<p>The transformation distribution in the Distill article is: pad 16 px, jitter up to 16 px, scale $\in\{1,0.975,1.025,0.95,1.05\}$, rotate $\in\{-5,\dots,5\}^\circ$, jitter up to 8 px, then crop. The defaults were 2560 steps of Adam at learning rate 0.05. Lucid's <code>standard_transforms</code> are: pad 12 (constant 0.5), jitter 8, scale $1+(i-5)/50$ for $i=0..10$, rotate $-10..10^\circ$ (extra weight on $0^\circ$), jitter 4. The essay: "It turned out the method was missing transformation robustness. Once researchers added it, the same neurons came out crisp."</p>
<p class="src">Source: Olah, Mordvintsev, Schubert 2017, https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/; lucid/optvis/transform.py (github.com/tensorflow/lucid); essay "A History of Microscope Errors".</p>

<h4>3.2 Decorrelated (Fourier + color) parameterization</h4>
<p>Optimizing raw pixels favors high frequencies. Lucid instead optimizes Fourier coefficients scaled by $1/f$, so all frequencies start with equal energy. It then maps a decorrelated color space to RGB with the square root of ImageNet's color covariance, and applies a sigmoid.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$P(\theta)=\sigma\!\Big(\tilde C\;\tfrac14\,\mathcal F^{-1}_{2D}\big[S\odot(\theta_{\Re}+i\,\theta_{\Im})\big]\Big),\qquad S(f)=\frac{\sqrt{WH}}{\max\!\big(|f|,\,1/\max(W,H)\big)^{d}},\;\; d=1$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\tilde C=\frac{C_{\sqrt{}}}{\max_k\|C_{\sqrt{},:k}\|_2},\qquad C_{\sqrt{}}=\begin{pmatrix}0.26\;\;0.09\;\;0.02\\0.27\;\;0.00\;\;-0.05\\0.27\;\;-0.09\;\;0.03\end{pmatrix}\;(\text{SVD square root of ImageNet RGB correlation})$$</div>
<p>The article describes this as a preconditioner. Gradient descent in a whitened basis amounts to steepest descent under a metric that does not over-reward high frequencies ("a good first guess is one that makes your data decorrelated and whitened").</p>
<p class="src">Source: lucid/optvis/param/spatial.py (<code>fft_image</code>, <code>rfft2d_freqs</code>) and param/color.py (<code>color_correlation_svd_sqrt</code>), github.com/tensorflow/lucid; https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/ ("Preconditioning and Parameterization").</p>

<h4>3.3 Regularizers</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$R_{\text{TV}}(x)=\sum_{h,w,c}\big|x_{h+1,w,c}-x_{h,w,c}\big|+\big|x_{h,w+1,c}-x_{h,w,c}\big|,\qquad R_{L1}(x)=\sum|x-c_0|$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$R_{\text{blur}}(x)=\tfrac12\big\|x-\mathrm{sg}[\mathrm{blur}(x)]\big\|_2^2\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; x\leftarrow(1-k)\,x+k\,\mathrm{blur}(x)\ \text{per step}$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{Diversity:}\quad G^{(a)}_{ij}=\sum_{h,w}z_\ell[h,w,i]\,z_\ell[h,w,j],\qquad C_{\text{div}}=-\sum_a\sum_{b\ne a}\cos\big(\mathrm{vec}\,G^{(a)},\mathrm{vec}\,G^{(b)}\big)$$</div>
<p>$\mathrm{sg}$ is stop-gradient. The essay credits deep-layer improvements to "a few subtle tweaks to reasonable settings" and "small regularization tweaks", but does not give the 2026 settings.</p>
<p class="src">Source: lucid/optvis/objectives.py (<code>total_variation</code>, <code>L1</code>, <code>blur_input_each_step</code>, <code>diversity</code>); https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/; essay.</p>

<h4>3.4 Faceted and attribution visualizations (Cammarata co-authored)</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{Faceted FV:}\quad \max_x\; f(g(x))+w^{\top}\big(g(x)\odot\nabla f(g(x))\big)$$</div>
<p>Here $g$ maps the input to a lower layer, $f$ is the original objective, and $w$ is a linear probe trained to separate a facet's images (text, face, pose, ...) from generic images. Multimodal Neurons reports that the gradient-gated term beats $f+w^\top g$.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{Attribution FV (Curve Detectors):}\quad \mathrm{FeatureVisualization}\big(\,|W\odot h_{\text{prev}}|\,\big)$$</div>
<p class="src">Source: Goh, Cammarata, Voss et al. 2021, https://distill.pub/2021/multimodal-neurons/ ("Faceted Feature Visualization"); Cammarata et al. 2020, https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/curve-detectors/.</p>

<h4>3.5 Failure mode: reward hacking in deep layers, and the path-anchored fix</h4>
<p>In late InceptionV1 layers, free optimization finds routes that natural images rarely use. "It takes the easiest path, which in InceptionV1 often means dogs ... the early optimization falls into them and never recovers." The fix is to bias early optimization toward the routes that real top examples use: "We took the neuron's top dataset examples, traced the paths they take, and gave the optimization an incentive to follow those paths, often only for the first 64 steps". The essay says "we don't have a canonical best one". It notes elsewhere that "the path weight is scaled to each unit's activation range".</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\nabla_x a_j=\phi'(z_j)\sum_i w_{ji}\,\nabla_x a_i\qquad(\text{the ascent direction is dominated by upstream units with large } w_{ji}\,\|\nabla_x a_i\|)$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{path-anchored:}\quad \mathcal J_s(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\big[a_j(x_\theta)\big]+\lambda_s\,\rho_j\,\cos\!\big(A_{\cdot\to j}(x_\theta),\,\bar r_j\big),\qquad \bar r_j=\tfrac1K\sum_{x\in D_j^+(K)}A_{\cdot\to j}(x),\quad \lambda_s=\lambda_0\,\mathbf 1[s\le 64]$$</div>
<p><span class="hyp">The first equation is the standard chain rule. The specific path term (cosine to the mean incoming attribution profile $\bar r_j$ of the top-$K$ examples, gated to the first 64 steps and scaled by the unit's activation range $\rho_j$) is our formalization. The essay describes the incentive only qualitatively. Our reading of the "dog basin" is a rich-get-richer ascent: InceptionV1 has many dog units with positive weights into late units, so the first steps amplify them, and the iterate settles in a basin far from the car route.</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: essay, main text "Cars and Animals" and appendix "Feature Visualization in Later Layers" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).</p>

<h3>4. Dataset examples, percentiles, poles, and the census</h3>

<h4>4.1 Positive and negative poles</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$s_j(x)=\max_{h,w} z_j(x)[h,w],;; s^{\min}_j(x)=\min_{h,w} z_j(x)[h,w],\qquad D_j^{+}(k)=\operatorname*{top\text{-}k}_{x\in\mathcal D}\,s_j(x),\qquad D_j^{-}(k)=\operatorname*{top\text{-}k}_{x\in\mathcal D}\,\big(-s^{\min}_j(x)\big)$$</div>
<p>$|\mathcal D|=50{,}000$ images in the essay's vision scans ("Each neuron's 20 strongest responses out of 50,000 images"; "Photos rank each neuron's image maxima over 50,000 training images"). The negative pole is the argmin set. The essay reads it as a gate that names the distinction being drawn ("the V neuron's negative side is an O"; "+ Taxis − Ambulances"). For ReLU nets, poles are read on pre-activations. Curve Detectors: "When studying ReLU networks, we find it helpful to look at the distribution of pre-activation values." For SwiGLU, see §8.</p>
<p class="src">Source: essay "How I Think About Neurons"; https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/curve-detectors/ ("Dataset Analysis").</p>

<h4>4.2 Percentiles vs raw activations</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$q_j(x)=\hat F_j\big(a_j(x)\big)=\frac1N\sum_{x'}\mathbf 1\big[a_j(x')\le a_j(x)\big],\qquad r_j(x)=\frac{a_j(x)}{\max_{x'}a_j(x')}$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{mean spacing between events above percentile } p:\;\; \frac{1}{1-p}\ \text{tokens}\;\Rightarrow\; p=0.99\!:\,100,\quad 0.999\!:\,10^3,\quad 0.9999\!:\,10^4$$</div>
<p>At about 400 tokens per page, these spacings match the essay's "1 token in 100 is about a quarter of a page ... the 99.9th percentile is every two or three pages, the 99.99th every 25 or 30". The essay prefers the raw shape $r_j$. "Percentiles flatten that, and it's often what tells you whether you're looking at a dial, where the middle matters, or a detector, where it mostly doesn't." For example, the Christmas neuron drops off "at about 5% of its max". Tails are exponential, not Gaussian: $p(z)\propto e^{-z/s}$ (Curve Detectors, crediting Brice Ménard). Curve Detectors weights the activation spectrum by contribution to expected value, $x\,p(x)$, or by $x\,\tfrac{d\,\text{logit}}{dx}\,p(x)$. Multimodal Neurons, which Cammarata co-authored, credits him with discovering "that negative pre-ReLU activations are often interpretable" and "that neurons sometimes contain a distinct regime change between medium and strong activations". Both are early forms of the essay's negative-pole and raw-shape views.</p>
<p class="src">Source: essay "Percentiles or raw activations?"; https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/curve-detectors/; https://distill.pub/2021/multimodal-neurons/.</p>

<h4>4.3 The "one main job" census</h4>
<p>The essay's headline census, for Qwen3-32B, is per neuron pole: "roughly 90% of neuron poles have one main job, often with small related offshoots; another ~7% have two related jobs; and only ~2-4% are not yet understood". Restricted to strong activations, it is "closer to 97% non-arbitrary mixtures" (small samples). The essay says the number depends on "the threshold for counting weak activations, depth, density of sub-circuits, and what you view as 'one thing'", and gives no formula.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$S_{j,\pm}(\tau)=\Big\{x:\ \pm a_j(x)\ \ge\ \tau\cdot\max_{x'}\big(\pm a_j(x')\big)\Big\},\qquad S_{j,\pm}(\tau)=\textstyle\bigsqcup_{m=1}^{M}J_m,\quad \pi_m=\frac{\sum_{x\in J_m}|a_j(x)|}{\sum_{x\in S}|a_j(x)|}$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{one main job}\iff \pi_1\ge\theta\ \wedge\ \forall m\gt 1:\ d(J_m,J_1)\le\delta;\qquad \text{two related jobs}\iff M_{\text{major}}=2\ \wedge\ d(J_1,J_2)\le\delta$$</div>
<p><span class="hyp">$\tau$, $\theta$, $\delta$, the clustering into jobs $J_m$ and the semantic distance $d$ are our placeholders. In the essay, an agent pipeline and judge do this step, writing 1,000 "neuron papers" on Qwen3-32B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B neurons.</span> The essay's tests of explanations are concrete. Blind maximum: a second agent sees only the explanation and writes an input that should maximize the neuron, and these inputs beat the best of 10B corpus tokens. Near-miss controls: for 200 random neurons, 183 with positive-side explanations, new examples fired strongly 76% of the time versus 11% for near misses (won 164, tied 15, lost 4). Earlier censuses for comparison: Multimodal Neurons, CLIP final conv layer, $n=50$: 76% interpretable (95% CI 64–88%), 18% polysemantic with interpretable facets, 6% uninterpretable. Bills et al. 2023: about 1.7% of GPT-2 XL neurons interpretable. Towards Monosemanticity: median neuron 0/14. GPT-2 XL re-audited: about 70% roughly one thing, about 5% pristine.</p>
<p class="src">Source: essay "The Short Version" footnote 1, "Explanations That Can Lose", "Slouching Towards Monosemanticity"; https://already-clean.goodfire.com/neuron-scientist/papers/; https://distill.pub/2021/multimodal-neurons/ (Guided Tour footnote).</p>

<h3>5. Attribution, ablation, steering</h3>

<h4>5.1 Gradient × activation and direct weights</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$A_{i\to y}(x)=a_i(x)\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial a_i}(x)\qquad\text{(Building Blocks: activation times gradient)}$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$A_{i\to j}(x)=a_i(x)\,\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial a_i}(x)\;\overset{\text{adjacent, linear }z_j}{=}\;W_{ji}\,a_i(x),\qquad \text{conv: }A_{i\to j}=\sum_{\Delta}W_{j,i,\Delta}\,a_i[\cdot+\Delta]$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{residual, LN gain frozen:}\quad W^{\text{virt}}_{ji}=\big(W_{\text{in}}^{(j)}\big)^{\!\top}\,\mathrm{diag}(g/\sigma)\,W_{\text{out}}^{(i)}$$</div>
<p>Building Blocks: "we estimate the effect of a neuron on the output is its activation times the rate at which increasing its activation increases the output." Curve Detectors: "The attribution tensor describing how all neurons in the previous layer influenced a given neuron is the activations pointwise multiplied by the weights". The essay reports edges as "mean routed attribution" over 8 examples, with a sign-agreement count (e.g. "+0.335768; 8/8 examples share the mean sign"). Its graph target is "the ground-truth class's one-vs-rest log-odds margin". <span class="hyp">The virtual-weight form across the residual stream is the standard frozen-LN linearization, not stated in the essay. "RelP", the essay's name for its graph method, is not defined in the text.</span></p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\bar A_{i\to j}=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{i\to j}(x_k),\qquad \text{agree}_{ij}=\#\{k:\operatorname{sign}A_{i\to j}(x_k)=\operatorname{sign}\bar A_{i\to j}\},\qquad m(x)=\ell_y-\log\sum_{c\ne y}e^{\ell_c}$$</div>
<p class="src">Source: Olah et al. 2018, https://distill.pub/2018/building-blocks/; https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/curve-detectors/; essay "Late Circuits", "Edge Featurizers".</p>

<h4>5.2 Attribution graphs and edge motifs</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$\mathcal G(x)=(V,E),\quad V=\{j: |a_j(x)|\ \text{large}\},\quad E=\{(i\!\to\! j,\ \bar A_{i\to j}) : |\bar A_{i\to j}|\ge\kappa\};\qquad \mathbf E\in\mathbb R^{n_{\text{graphs}}\times n_{\text{edges}}}\approx UV$$</div>
<p>The essay reports "Factoring 25,000 ImageNet attribution graphs gave us connected circuits of ordinary neurons", for example a teapot motif. It finds that ablation damage tracks motif usage (correlations up to $\rho=0.80$ across motifs). <span class="hyp">The graph-by-edge matrix and the low-rank factorization $UV$ are our rendering. The essay does not name the factorization method.</span> It also clusters incoming attribution into "bundles" and runs one blind maximum per bundle with the other bundles ablated.</p>
<p class="src">Source: essay "Edge Featurizers", "Explanations That Can Lose", "Audits of Attribution Graphs".</p>

<h4>5.3 Ablation, weak-activation removal, steering</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{ablate:}\ h_i\leftarrow 0,\qquad \textbf{effect: }\Delta P=P(y\mid \mathrm{do}(h_S=0))-P(y);\qquad \textbf{steer:}\ h_i\leftarrow h_i+\alpha\quad(\text{or } h_i\leftarrow \alpha\,\max h_i)$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{weak-activation removal:}\ h_i\leftarrow h_i\,\mathbf 1\big[|h_i|\ge\tau_i\big],\ \tau_i=Q_{0.75}(|h_i|)\qquad \textbf{extreme removal:}\ \text{out}_i\leftarrow 0\ \text{if}\ g_i\gt Q^{\text{ordinary}}_{0.999}(g_i)$$</div>
<p>Reported numbers. Medium ConvNeXt, all 18 blocks: removing the weakest 75% gives 72%→71% accuracy with 1 in 10 answers changed. Removing the strongest 7% ("the same total activation as the weakest 75%") gives 72%→4%. InceptionV1 final layer, keeping about the strongest quarter of positive activations: 69.47% vs 70.08%. Qwen: removing 8 Santa negative-pole neurons drops Santa to 6%; removing the 20 neurons labeled Christmas/Santa leaves it at 76%. Zeroing the Poe neuron moves P(" dre") from 87% to 50%. Kimi K3: removing one "holds back" neuron moves P(return) from 0.83 to 0.98. Gate above its 99.9th percentile zeroed: Psalm 23 recitation fails. Steering: the Colorado comma neuron "made the model want to go hiking". <span class="hyp">The quantile threshold $\tau_i$ is our reading of "weakest 75%". Whether that is per neuron or global is not stated.</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: essay "Do Weak Activations Matter?", appendix "Weak Activations: Further Measurements", "Exploring Language Models", "Memorization and Composition", "Superposition of the Gaps" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).</p>

<h3>6. Sparse autoencoders and the near-copy comparison</h3>
<div class="eq-block">$$z=\mathrm{TopK}_k\big(W_{\text{enc}}(x-b_{\text{pre}})\big),\qquad \hat x=W_{\text{dec}}z+b_{\text{pre}},\qquad \mathcal L=\|x-\hat x\|_2^2+\alpha\,\|e-\hat e\|_2^2,\;\; \hat e=W_{\text{dec}}\,\mathrm{TopK}_{k_{\text{aux}}}(z_{\text{dead}})$$</div>
<p>The TopK SAE of Gao et al. 2024 has an auxiliary loss that reconstructs the residual $e=x-\hat x$ from dead latents. The essay's crushed-layer SAE: 640 features, $k=2$, auxiliary loss on, 25.6M positions from 200,000 ImageNet images. It reaches 82% held-out variance explained ($\mathrm{FVU}=0.18$), and spliced in, it drops top-1 from 58.8% to 43.0%. Matched to the original curve detectors (24–56° wide, silent at the opposite angle), the best SAE features are 44–174° wide and two-sided, with shape correlation 0.38–0.79. An SAE on the original layer matches at 0.96–1.00.</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$c_f=\max_i\big|\cos(d_f,e_i)\big|,\qquad \text{near-copy}(f)\iff c_f\ge\kappa,\qquad \text{share}=\frac{\sum_{f:\,\text{near-copy}}\mathbb E[z_f]}{\sum_f\mathbb E[z_f]}\;=\;0.88\ (\text{essay, last layer})$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{curve-neuron share of SAE feature }f:\ \ \frac{\sum_{i\in\text{curve}}|d_{f,i}|}{\sum_i|d_{f,i}|}\in\{97.5\%,98.0\%,97.2\%\}\ (\text{essay examples})$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{rotation search:}\quad R^\star=\arg\max_{R\in SO(n)}\ \mathbb E_x\Big[\textstyle\sum_i (R\,h(x))_i^4\Big]\ \Rightarrow\ R^\star\approx\text{signed permutation (the neuron basis)}$$</div>
<p>Quotes: "On the last layer, 88% of its activity is near-copies of single neurons, and the rest is faint: removing every dictionary direction that matches no neuron leaves accuracy unchanged". Also: "if you search the rotations of a layer for the one where a handful of units carry the most activity, starting from a random one, you land back on the neurons, in every block." <span class="hyp">The decoder–neuron cosine $c_f$, threshold $\kappa$, $\mathbb E[z_f]$ as "activity", the $L_1$ share formula and the kurtosis-style rotation objective are our formalizations. The essay does not give them.</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: Gao et al. 2024, "Scaling and Evaluating Sparse Autoencoders", arXiv:2406.04341; essay "What Dictionaries Learn", "What Neurons Do Under Pressure", appendix "Missing Curves"; Gorton 2024 arXiv:2406.03662.</p>

<h3>7. Superposition vs recursive generalization</h3>

<h4>7.1 The toy model of superposition</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$h=Wx,\qquad x'=\mathrm{ReLU}(W^{\top}h+b)=\mathrm{ReLU}(W^{\top}Wx+b),\qquad \mathcal L=\sum_x\sum_i I_i\,(x_i-x'_i)^2$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$x_i=0\ \text{w.p. } S_i,\ \text{else } x_i\sim U[0,1];\qquad D_i=\frac{\|W_i\|^2}{\sum_j(\hat W_i\cdot W_j)^2}\qquad(\text{interference: off-diagonal } (W^\top W)_{ij})$$</div>
<p>The essay's critique: the toy "prices the cost of sharing a neuron, interference, but not the benefit, reuse", because "every feature is an end in itself, and nothing downstream builds on what two features share". The essay's modified toy has 20 inputs in 4 correlated groups of 5, a 5-dimensional bottleneck, and an added layer of hidden neurons. In it, "related inputs grew onto the neurons". The share of bottleneck weight along the data's top principal components was 0.37 (original toy, density 0.1), 0.80 (40 hidden neurons, density 0.1) and 0.79 (original toy, dense 0.9, PCA-like).</p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{PC share}(W)=\frac{\|W P_k\|_F^2}{\|W\|_F^2},\qquad P_k=\text{projector onto top-}k\text{ principal components of }\mathrm{Cov}(x)$$</div>
<p><span class="hyp">The PC-share formula is our reading of "share of the bottleneck's weights along the data's top principal components".</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: Elhage et al. 2022, https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html; essay appendix "Toy Models".</p>

<h4>7.2 Two opposite predictions</h4>
<p>The essay frames generalization and superposition as making opposite, testable predictions: "generalization should be local, superposition should be non-local". "Generalization would want a floppy ear dog neuron ... Superposition would want a floppy ear dog + Latin poetry neuron." <span class="hyp">With a semantic distance $d$ and co-occurrence probability $P(u\wedge v)$ over the members of a neuron's receptive set $S_j$:</span></p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\textbf{generalization:}\ \ \mathbb E_{u,v\in S_j}\,d(u,v)\ \text{small};\qquad \textbf{superposition:}\ \ S_j=\arg\min_{S}\sum_{u\ne v\in S}P(u\wedge v)\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \mathbb E_{u,v\in S_j}\,d(u,v)\ \text{large}$$</div>
<p class="src">Source: essay "Superposition" and "Superposition of the Gaps" (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).</p>

<h4>7.3 Recursive generalization (our formalization)</h4>
<p>The essay's definition: "a neuron generalizing along more than one axis, often first along one axis, then sprouting outwards in ways that are easy to follow locally, but stop it from being 'monosemantic' in the end". Example: "iy" → "diy" → "do it yourself". <span class="hyp">Let each input $u$ have a set of facets $\phi(u)$ (sub-features such as the letters "iy", the meaning DIY, yellow, field). Neuron $j$'s receptive set at training time $t$ is $S_j(t)=\{u: a_j^{(t)}(u)\ge\tau\}$. Recursive generalization is the growth rule below.</span></p>
<div class="eq-block">$$u'\in S_j(t+1)\setminus S_j(t)\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists\,u\in S_j(t):\ \phi(u)\cap\phi(u')\ne\varnothing$$</div>
<p><span class="hyp">So the final set $S_j(T)$ is path-connected under the facet-sharing graph and rooted at the seed $S_j(t_0)$. Its endpoints need not share any facet: $\phi(\text{"iy"})\cap\phi(\text{"do it yourself"})=\varnothing$ while each adjacent pair overlaps. Single-concept monosemanticity fails, but the set has a narrow, checkable shape in both semantic space and time. That is the essay's "specific shape in both semantic space and time, and a narrow target to hit by chance". The mechanism the essay proposes ("A neuron that fires on 'iy' is already on for 'diy,' so it's first in line") follows from the ReLU gradient in §1.4. On a new input $u'$, only neurons with $z_j(u')\gt 0$ receive weight updates, and the update is proportional to their current activation of shared inputs.</span></p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\Delta w_j\ \propto\ -\,\delta_j(u')\,\mathbf 1[z_j(u')\gt 0]\,a_{\text{in}}(u')\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{neurons already partly on for }u'\text{ (shared facet) are recruited first}$$</div>
<p><span class="hyp">A linear model of the essay's "circuit race" (the Labrador example). Two circuits, broad ($b$) and exact ($e$), share the residual error and receive gradient in proportion to their coverage $c_b\gg c_e$ (fraction of the input they fire on):</span></p>
<div class="eq-block">$$\epsilon(t)=y-u_b(t)-u_e(t),\qquad \dot u_k=\eta\,c_k\,\epsilon(t)\ \Rightarrow\ \epsilon(t)=y\,e^{-\eta(c_b+c_e)t},\qquad u_k(\infty)=\frac{c_k}{c_b+c_e}\,y$$</div>
<p><span class="hyp">The broad circuit captures a fraction $c_b/(c_b+c_e)$ of the target, and the exact circuit "starves" because the shared error vanishes. On the next example the broad circuit starts partly on, which is the rich-get-richer step. This matches coherent gradients (Chatterjee 2020): per-example gradients $g=\sum_x g_x$ add along shared directions.</span> The essay's whole-network version: a blank 7.1M model alone reaches 48–50% on Flowers-102, but trained jointly with a pretrained twin on a shared objective it reaches about 2%, while the twin takes 92.8–97.4% of the "marginal objective credit".</p>
<p class="src">Source: essay "Recursive Generalization", appendices "Why Networks Generalize", "Circuit Racing", "More on Circuit Ecology", "Is Recursive Generalization Hard to Vary?"; Multimodal Neurons "conjoined features" (https://distill.pub/2021/multimodal-neurons/: concepts "overlapping in a superficial way in one facet, and then generalizing out in multiple directions").</p>

<h4>7.4 Measuring it: watching neurons over training</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$\text{share}_{j,g}(t)=\frac{\big|D^{+}_j(k;\theta_t)\cap G_g\big|}{k},\qquad \text{locality}(t)=\min_{u\in D_j^+(k;\theta_{t-1})} d\big(u_{\text{new}},u\big)\ \ \text{vs}\ \ d\big(u_{\text{new}},\bar u_{\text{seed}}\big)$$</div>
<p>The essay tracks the same coordinate across checkpoints of a retrained network. For InceptionV1 neuron 338 at epochs 0.7, 1.5, 3 and 12, image groups $G_g$ shift from rapeseed fields and valleys toward tennis balls, terriers and broccoli. ConvNeXt neuron 399 stays rapeseed. InceptionV1 mixed5a:178 is tracked at steps 0 to 30,000. The plotted "grey band behind each row is its share of the neuron's strongest images", which is $\text{share}_{j,g}(t)$. <span class="hyp">The locality statistic is our proposal. Recursive generalization predicts that new members are near some existing member even when they are far from the seed's centroid. Superposition predicts new members far from all existing members.</span> The squeeze experiments are the static counterpart. With mixed3b cut from 480 to 10 channels (48×) and no bypass, the neurons become "coarser" ("one 'curves &amp; loops' neuron instead of ten curve detectors"), not mixed: "Fewer neurons make generalizing neurons coarser, never fractured."</p>
<p class="src">Source: essay "Recursive Generalization" figure, appendix "Watching Neurons Develop", "What Neurons Do Under Pressure".</p>

<h3>8. SwiGLU and the double-negative regime</h3>
<div class="eq-block">$$h=\mathrm{SiLU}(W_g x)\odot(W_v x),\qquad \text{out}=W_o h,\qquad a=\mathrm{SiLU}(g)\cdot v,\qquad \mathrm{SiLU}(g)=g\,\sigma(g)\ge -0.278$$</div>
<div class="eq-block">$$a=\underbrace{a\,\mathbf 1[g\gt 0,v\gt 0]}_{(+,+)}+\underbrace{a\,\mathbf 1[g\lt 0,v\lt 0]}_{(-,-)\ \text{double negative}}+\underbrace{a\,\mathbf 1[g\gt 0,v\lt 0]}_{(+,-)}+\underbrace{a\,\mathbf 1[g\lt 0,v\gt 0]}_{(-,+)},\qquad (-,-):\ 0\lt a\le 0.278\,|v|$$</div>
<p>The essay: "When both are negative, the output is positive, so a neuron can fire for two quite different reasons." "A negative gate leaves a small negative factor. Multiplying it by a negative value produces a positive activation." The double-negative "echo" "usually look[s] like the negative pole". "Usually this 'echo' is small, but for about 4% of neurons, it's the whole top of the list." Ablating these across the whole network "ups most benchmarks, but lowers some reasoning ones". In the Qwen3-32B L1/N20736 example, every top-list firing has the gate closed, "a small sign-flipped leak of the value, under 8% of the neuron's peak". With the leak separated, the neuron's job is "the token that completes 'Russia' or 'Russian,' in any script". In vision SwiGLU, "the value tends to learn broader things, and gate more specific things". <span class="hyp">The bound $a\le 0.278|v|$ in the $(-,-)$ regime follows from $\min\mathrm{SiLU}=-0.278$ (at $g\approx-1.28$). Why it fools top-$k$ dashboards: when $|v|$ is large on the value's negative side, $0.278|v|$ can exceed the $(+,+)$ responses of a sparse, narrow gate. The fix the essay uses is to report poles per regime, $D^{+}_{j}$ restricted to $\mathbf 1[g\gt 0]$.</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: Shazeer 2020 (GLU variants); essay "Exploring Language Models" (SwiGLU paragraph), appendix "Separating the Sign Regimes" (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B L36/E206/N103, 750 quantile-stratified examples, 626 retained routing) (https://already-clean.goodfire.com/).</p>

<h4>8.1 Mixture-of-experts as "anti-superposition" (essay argument)</h4>
<div class="eq-block">$$p(e\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}(W_r x)_e,\qquad \text{neuron } j\in e\ \text{fires only if } e\in\operatorname{TopK}_e\,p(e\mid x)$$</div>
<p>The essay argues that "the router would have to recognize all ten thousand to know when to send a token there, and the router is just one small linear layer". <span class="hyp">Formally, an expert receives the token set $\{x: w_e^\top x\ge \max_{e'\ne e}w_{e'}^\top x\}$ (for top-1), which is a convex polyhedral cone. Packing many unrelated, semantically scattered items into one expert requires them to lie in one such cone, while the related items that are not packed must lie outside it. A linear router makes that unlikely.</span></p>
<p class="src">Source: essay "Exploring Language Models" (expert specialization paragraph).</p>
